\section{Introduction}

Making a server fault-tolerant without rewriting it---\emph{transparent} fault
tolerance---has been a recurring goal of systems research for four decades, and it
has repeatedly failed to reach production. The reason was never the snapshot.
Checkpointing a process is well understood. The reason was three \emph{coupled}
costs that a transparent system must pay on the critical path, and that were all a
large fraction of application latency at the operating point every prior system
lived at:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Logging non-determinism.} To replay a recovered replica to the exact
  state of the failed one, every non-deterministic event---above all the
  \emph{order} in which concurrent messages were processed---must be recorded.
  Order logging is the dominant per-event overhead of deterministic
  replay~\cite{mashtizadeh2017castor,ocallahan2017rr}.
\item \textbf{Coordinating a consistent cut.} A snapshot of a distributed system
  must be globally consistent; the classical mechanism is Chandy--Lamport marker
  propagation~\cite{chandy1985snapshots}, which captures in-flight channel state.
\item \textbf{Output commit.} Output that has become externally visible cannot be
  un-sent on recovery, so a transparent system must \emph{withhold} each
  externalizing message until the state that produced it is durable
  \cite{strom1985optimistic}. This output-hold is what sank Remus~\cite{cully2008remus}: tens of
  milliseconds of latency added to every client reply.
\end{enumerate}

Faced with these costs, the industry chose the \emph{other} path: rewrite
applications so that state is externalized into a system that is fault-tolerant by
construction---durable execution (Temporal, DBOS, Restate~\cite{temporal}) and
exactly-once stream processing (Flink~\cite{carbone2015flink}). That path won mindshare, but
it won it one rewrite at a time. The legacy fleet---millions of lines of stock
Redis, Memcached, Nginx, application servers, and databases---was left behind,
because rewriting them is exactly the cost transparent FT was meant to avoid.

\paragraph{This paper.} We revisit the abandoned transparent path and show that it
becomes practical under two co-designed shifts, neither sufficient alone.

The \textbf{first} shift is a user-space \emph{determinism libOS}: an
\texttt{LD\_PRELOAD} shim, with no kernel and no application changes, that makes an
unmodified server deterministically recoverable by virtualizing its residual
\emph{local} non-determinism---reads of the clock, of the random source, and the
order of thread scheduling. We show that for real servers the residual local
non-determinism, once the message order is supplied externally, is small and
closable: stock Redis, Memcached, Nginx, Node.js, and a multi-process PostgreSQL
all recover \emph{byte-identically} across a crash and a real-time gap, at a
few-percent steady-state overhead.

The \textbf{second} shift is the operating point. Prior transparent systems paid
the three costs over software-ordered, TCP-scale, hypervisor-logged
\emph{millisecond} paths, where each was a large fraction of request latency. If
instead all inter-process communication of \emph{share-nothing} servers is routed
through an in-network total-order \emph{reliable} fabric---1Pipe~\cite{li2021onepipe},
which establishes total order in a programmable switch and delivers reliably via a
two-phase commit barrier, over an RDMA fabric whose round-trip is $1$--$2\,\mu s$---then
the three costs do not merely shrink; they \emph{change structure}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item the fabric \emph{is} the order, so the replay order-log vanishes;
\item a timestamp-$T$ snapshot replaces the Chandy--Lamport cut---and is in fact
  stronger, because an order-preserving fabric has no in-flight channel state to
  capture; and
\item the output-commit barrier \emph{is} the fabric's reliable-delivery commit
  barrier, so the durable-replica write completes under a barrier the system already
  pays for, and fault tolerance adds no round trip over the reliable-fabric baseline.
\end{itemize}
The single new engineering idea is the last point: OneBarrier replicates its
recovery log using 1Pipe's native 1-RTT replication---scattering each input to the
backup replicas at its total-order position---while the output-commit hold waits for
the reliable-delivery commit barrier (1.5 RTT). The one-RTT replication completes
\emph{within} that barrier, so the recovery state is durable by the time output may
be released, and fault tolerance adds no round trip over the reliable-fabric
baseline. At the millisecond scale this overlap saves little; at $1$--$2\,\mu s$ RTT
it is the difference between fault tolerance being a tax on every reply and being a
byproduct of the communication fabric.

\paragraph{Scope, stated up front.} This is a realization-and-mea\-surement result, not
a new primitive, and three boundaries define it precisely. (i) The conceptual idea is
anticipated: LLFT~\cite{zhao2013llft} did transparent, order-log-free FT of unmodified
socket applications using totally-ordered virtual time, and HyCoR~\cite{zhou2021hycor}
did checkpoint-replay of unmodified containers. The contribution is the co-design with
an \emph{in-network} fabric and the demonstration that the cost \emph{regime} changes at
the microsecond operating point; \S\ref{sec:novelty} states the prior art and what
survives. (ii) The libOS, the determinism, the recovery, the correctness checks, and
the resource costs are measured on stock binaries. The barrier coincidence is an
architectural identity that holds by construction (\S\ref{sec:overview}); its absolute
\emph{magnitude} on RDMA is established by a discrete-event model at the published
operating point and corroborated by real RDMA verbs over software RoCE, not by a
hardware testbed (\S\ref{sec:eval-overlap}). (iii) Durability is in-memory $f$-of-$k$
fail-stop tolerance via in-fabric replication, not crash-consistent persistence across
correlated power loss---the FaRM/RAMCloud tradeoff~\cite{dragojevic2014farm}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A transparent determinism libOS that closes the local-non-determinism
  boundary for real unmodified servers---time via a virtual clock, randomness via
  syscall-level interception, and threads via share-nothing sharding---and recovers
  stock Redis, Memcached, Nginx, and Node.js \emph{byte-identically by deterministic
  replay}, plus a multi-process PostgreSQL via the general CRIU checkpoint/restore path
  (\S\ref{sec:libos}, \S\ref{sec:eval-recovery}).
\item A structural argument, and its quantification, that routing unmodified
  share-nothing servers through an in-network total-order reliable fabric collapses the
  three coupled costs of transparent FT by construction---the output-commit hold and
  the fabric's reliable-delivery barrier are the same barrier, so FT adds no round trip
  (\S\ref{sec:overview}, \S\ref{sec:eval-overlap}).
\item An end-to-end evaluation: byte-identical recovery and state reconstruction;
  linearizability and exactly-once under injected crashes; recovery latency that is
  affine in log length and bounded by checkpointing (including CRIU of a
  multi-process database); steady-state overhead; and head-to-head resource costs
  against HyCoR, LLFT, active SMR, and CRIU (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
\item A generality study extending the mechanism, via an explicit fit-test, to ten more
  applications across five classes---message brokers (Redis Streams, a Kafka partition
  model, and Mosquitto MQTT), databases (SQLite on the deterministic-replay path,
  MySQL/MariaDB on the CRIU path), a software network function (an OpenClickNP
  load-balancer with connection tracking), a stateful microservice, and
  network-infrastructure servers (dnsmasq, lighttpd, HAProxy)---each recovering
  exactly-once and byte-identically, including a small libOS extension for UDP servers
  (\S\ref{sec:ext}).
\item Machine-checked TLA+ specifications of 1Pipe total order and of OneBarrier's
  exactly-once recovery, and a candid novelty reckoning (\S\ref{sec:impl},
  \S\ref{sec:novelty}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Why Transparent FT Failed, and What Changed}
\label{sec:why}

\paragraph{The three coupled costs.} A transparent FT system must, on every
externalizing message, guarantee that the producing state is recoverable. Three
mechanisms make that guarantee, and historically each was expensive.
\emph{Deterministic replay} reconstructs a failed replica's state by re-executing
its inputs in the same order; the order itself is non-deterministic and must be
logged, and message-order logging dominates replay overhead~\cite{mashtizadeh2017castor}. A
\emph{consistent distributed snapshot} is needed so that a recovered replica
resumes from a globally coherent point; Chandy--Lamport~\cite{chandy1985snapshots}
achieves this by injecting markers and recording channel state. And
\emph{output commit}~\cite{strom1985optimistic} forbids releasing output until its
producing state is durable, because external observers cannot be rolled back. Remus
\cite{cully2008remus} paid this last cost as tens of milliseconds of buffered output per
externalizing message; VMware FT~\cite{scales2010vmwareft} paid the order cost as
lock-step execution. At the millisecond scale, all three were a large fraction of
request latency---which is why transparent FT, despite four decades of work, never
displaced application-level replication.

\paragraph{The 1Pipe fabric.} 1Pipe~\cite{li2021onepipe} provides \emph{scalable total
order} communication in a datacenter: messages carry timestamps, an in-network
(P4 programmable switch) barrier aggregation establishes a global order, and a FIFO
gate at each receiver delivers in timestamp order. A \emph{scattering}---a group of
messages to different destinations at one total-order position---has best-effort
semantics ($0.5$ RTT plus barrier wait, adding up to $10\,\mu s$) or reliable
semantics. Reliable delivery is a two-phase commit with in-network barrier
aggregation: the first phase is end-to-end packet-loss recovery, and the second
aggregates a \emph{commit barrier} that makes a scattering's delivery atomic
(all-or-nothing); it takes $1.5$ RTT plus barrier wait, one RTT more than
best-effort, adding up to $21\,\mu s$. 1Pipe also provides 1-RTT replication
natively: a client scatters a log record to all replicas at a single total-order
position, and each replica orders by (timestamp, source). At its operating
point---RDMA RTT $1$--$2\,\mu s$, $80$\,M messages/s/host, replication in one RTT,
and failure detection in tens of microseconds---the per-message ordering and
reliable-commit costs are set by the fabric, not by software.

\paragraph{The operating-point argument.} Our central claim is not that the idea is
new but that the \emph{cost regime} is. The three costs that doomed transparent FT
were properties of a software-ordered, TCP-scale, millisecond world. Once order is
established in the switch and durable replication completes in one RTT under a barrier
the communication layer already crosses, the three costs are no longer mechanisms added
on the FT critical path: each is a structural consequence of the fabric, deployed for
communication-correctness reasons and paid whether or not the system is
fault-tolerant. This is a design conclusion, not a hardware speedup. The argument is
architectural and holds by construction---the order \emph{is} the fabric's order, the
snapshot cut \emph{is} a fabric timestamp, the durability barrier \emph{is} the
fabric's commit barrier---and the absolute round-trip time sets only the magnitude of a
margin that is already zero in structure. Transparent passive FT at the
reliable-fabric baseline is the consequence, and it is the consequence at any RTT; the
microsecond operating point is what makes the consequence \emph{matter}.

\section{Overview: Fault Tolerance as a Byproduct of the Fabric}
\label{sec:overview}

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the architecture. An unmodified share-nothing server
runs over the determinism libOS, which intercepts its POSIX surface: sockets are
routed to the 1Pipe fabric, and local non-determinism (time, randomness, thread
order) is virtualized so that, given the same input sequence, the server's
execution is a deterministic function of that sequence. The fabric supplies the
input order and a reliable-delivery commit barrier; an in-fabric replica supplies
durability, because each input is scattered to it in one RTT and is durable by the
time the commit barrier passes.

The fabric pre-pays, for communication-correctness reasons, for exactly the three
things FT needs. Each collapse below is a structural identity, not an optimization: the
mechanism FT requires and the mechanism the fabric already runs are the same mechanism
(Figure~\ref{fig:threecosts}).

\paragraph{(a) Order $\Rightarrow$ no replay order-log.} Because 1Pipe delivers
messages in a global timestamp order, the recovered replica re-applies inputs in
\texttt{msg\_ts} order with no recorded order-log. The dominant cost of
deterministic replay is removed not by optimization but by relocation: the order
lives in the fabric. This is the state-machine-replication property~\cite{schneider1990smr}
realized over an in-network order source.

\paragraph{(b) Timestamp-$T$ snapshot $\Rightarrow$ empty-channel cut.} To snapshot,
the libOS quiesces at a barrier timestamp $T$: it stops dispatching inputs with
\texttt{ts}~$>T$, drains in-flight handlers for \texttt{ts}~$\le T$, and checkpoints.
Every message is, by the identical \texttt{ts}~$\le T$ predicate, on the same side
of the cut at sender and receiver, so the cut is consistent with \emph{no} marker
propagation and \emph{no} captured channel state---strictly simpler than
Chandy--Lamport, because an order-preserving fabric has no in-flight channel state.
The cut is uncoordinated: each node applies the same predicate independently.

\paragraph{(c) Output-commit $=$ commit barrier.} A server's reply is withheld until
the producing state is durable. In OneBarrier the recovery log is replicated by
scattering each input to the backup replicas at its total-order position---1Pipe's
1-RTT replication---and the reply is held until the reliable-delivery commit barrier
(the second phase of 1Pipe's 2PC, which makes the scattering atomic) passes the
input's timestamp. The fabric crosses that $\sim\!1.5$-RTT barrier to deliver the input
reliably \emph{regardless of fault tolerance}, and the 1-RTT replication completes
inside it. These are not two barriers that happen to overlap: the output-commit hold
and the reliable-delivery barrier are the \emph{same} barrier. FT therefore adds no
round trip over the reliable-fabric baseline---a margin that is zero by construction,
not a small measured number, because the durable write finishes within a wait the
fabric already performs. The absolute RTT sets how large the avoided cost would have
been; it does not set the margin, which is structurally zero. \S\ref{sec:eval-overlap}
quantifies the magnitude.

What makes (c) work in practice is (a)+(b)+the libOS: the recovered replica is a
deterministic function of the fabric-ordered input prefix up to the last durable
barrier, so ``the producing state is durable'' reduces to ``the input prefix is
replicated,'' which the fabric already does.

\section{A Transparent Determinism libOS for Unmodified Servers}
\label{sec:libos}

The fabric supplies order; the libOS must supply everything else needed for an
\emph{unmodified} binary to replay deterministically. This section is the
mechanism that makes ``transparent'' real. The libOS is three composable
\texttt{LD\_PRELOAD} libraries totalling $\sim$900 lines of C, in the
SocksDirect~\cite{li2019socksdirect} kernel-bypass lineage; it requires no kernel
module and no application change.

\subsection{The determinism boundary}
Once message order is external, a server's remaining non-determinism is
\emph{local}: values it reads from the clock and the random source, and---if
multi-threaded---the order its threads interleave. We found, and the libOS closes,
a sharp boundary between two kinds of time reads. \emph{Request-driven} reads (one
per request, e.g.\ a timestamp stamped into a reply) replay correctly by sequence
position. \emph{Timer-driven} reads (a background timer firing on wall-clock, e.g.\
Redis's \texttt{serverCron}, Nginx's cached-time update, Memcached's LRU
maintainer) fire a \emph{different number of times} between record and replay,
desynchronizing any position-indexed scheme. The virtual clock closes this boundary
by making time a function of input events rather than of wall-clock or read count
(Figure~\ref{fig:boundary}). One time path remains outside the boundary: a binary that
reads the timestamp counter directly via the \texttt{RDTSC} instruction takes no
syscall and exports no symbol, so neither \texttt{LD\_PRELOAD} nor the syscall trap
catches it---the time analog of the \texttt{RDRAND} hole we close for randomness
(\S\ref{sec:rng}). None of our five servers read \texttt{RDTSC} for
externally-visible time (their reads go through the libc surface the virtual clock
intercepts), but a general guarantee would pin it with \texttt{prctl(PR\_SET\_TSC)} to
trap-and-emulate \texttt{RDTSC} from the virtual clock; we mark this as the residual
escape hatch (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

\caption{The determinism boundary. Under wall-clock time a background timer fires a
different number of times between record and replay, so timer-driven reads desync

\subsection{Virtual clock: time virtualization}
The libOS intercepts the libc time surface (\texttt{gettimeofday},
\texttt{clock\_gettime}, \texttt{time}) and returns \emph{virtual} time
$t = \mathrm{base} + \mathrm{ticks}$, where \texttt{ticks} advances a fixed delta
per socket read---i.e.\ per deterministic input event. Every time read is then
\emph{count-independent}: it does not matter how many times, or from which timer,
the value is read, only how many inputs have been processed. \texttt{base} is
captured at the live run and persisted; the recovered run reconstructs the identical
virtual time for the identical input sequence. The mechanism is app-agnostic---it
catches any binary's libc time calls, and the vDSO is not an escape hatch because
\texttt{LD\_PRELOAD} overrides the exported symbols (a control counted exactly
$2\!\times\!10^6$ intercepted \texttt{gettimeofday} and \texttt{clock\_gettime}
calls). Listing~\ref{lst:vclock} shows the mechanism in full. The sharpest
demonstration is Nginx: the HTTP \texttt{Date} header, formatted deep inside Nginx
from its cached time, is byte-identical on the live and recovered instances across a
multi-second real-time gap.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=cstyle,caption={The virtual clock: time advances one tick
per input event, so every time read is a deterministic function of the input
prefix.},label={lst:vclock},float=t]
ssize_t recv(int fd, void *b, size_t n, int fl) {
  ssize_t r = real_recv(fd, b, n, fl);
  if (r > 0 && is_conn(fd))            // a deterministic input event:
    atomic_fetch_add(&ticks, TICK_NS); //   advance virtual time one tick
  return r;
}
int clock_gettime(clockid_t c, struct timespec *t) {
  long long v = base_ns + atomic_load(&ticks);  // base persisted at record
  t->tv_sec = v / 1000000000; t->tv_nsec = v % 1000000000;
  return 0;                            // count-independent: same inputs -> same time
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Randomness virtualization}
\label{sec:rng}
A server's randomness reaches it by paths that defeat library interposition.
glibc's \texttt{getrandom} wrapper is interceptable, but V8/Node.js, OpenSSL, and
\texttt{arc4random} issue the \emph{raw} \texttt{getrandom} syscall, and Redis~6
seeds its dictionary hash (SipHash) by reading \texttt{/dev/urandom} through
\texttt{fopen}, whose internal \texttt{openat}/\texttt{read} bypass the public
symbols. The libOS therefore intercepts at the syscall level: a seccomp-BPF
user-notification filter traps \texttt{getrandom} and fills the caller's buffer from
a deterministic stream seeded from a persisted seed; \texttt{/dev/urandom} is
redirected, via a private mount namespace, to a deterministic file. Two further
entropy sources must be pinned for V8's \texttt{Math.random}: address-space layout
(disabled with \texttt{setarch -R}) and the \texttt{RDRAND} CPU instruction, which
no syscall trap can catch (disabled for OpenSSL via \texttt{OPENSSL\_ia32cap}). With
the full stack, Node's \texttt{Date.now()} \emph{and} \texttt{Math.random()} are
byte-identical across recovery, and Redis's \texttt{SPOP}/\texttt{SRANDMEMBER}
outputs---which depend on the SipHash-seeded dictionary layout---recover identically
where a control with the real device differs entirely.

\subsection{Threads: deterministic scheduling vs.\ share-nothing sharding}
\label{sec:threads}
For a multi-threaded server the OS chooses the order threads enter critical
sections, so shared-state evolution is non-deterministic. We implemented a
Kendo-style~\cite{olszewski2009kendo} deterministic scheduler: a thread may take a
top-level lock only when its logical clock is the global minimum, making the interleaving a
function of the clocks rather than of OS timing. It is correct---a 4-thread
microbenchmark's critical-section order is identical across runs where it otherwise
varies---but it serializes critical sections and \emph{collapses throughput} on a
contended server. The fundamental cost is the serialization of critical sections; an
implementation artifact compounds it, because our spin-based turn gating busy-waits a
4-worker Memcached into a $>\!1000\times$ slowdown ($448\%$ CPU spinning). A
blocking turn-handoff would soften the constant factor but not the
serialization---the same throughput wall the deterministic-multithreading literature
reports~\cite{olszewski2009kendo,bergan2010coredet} and that execute-then-verify SMR
(Eve~\cite{kapritsos2012eve}, Rex~\cite{guo2014rex}) sidesteps only by relaxing strict
determinism. The practical answer is not deterministic scheduling but the
\emph{share-nothing sharding} model that high-performance servers already use: run $N$
\emph{single-thread} instances, each deterministic by construction (one thread, so
input order is whatever the fabric supplies and there are no data races), and scale
by processes, not threads. Because shards share nothing, they have no lock
contention: four single-thread Memcached shards reach $1.0$\,M ops/s aggregate,
exceeding one 4-worker process (821\,k ops/s), while staying deterministic. The
deterministic scheduler remains as the fallback for genuinely shared mutable state
(Figures~\ref{fig:detsched} and~\ref{fig:sharding}).

Single-thread servers still spawn timer-driven maintenance threads---Memcached's LRU
maintainer mutates LRU bookkeeping on a real-time schedule---which we disable so the
shard's state evolves purely from the request stream and the virtual clock.

\subsection{Process-state checkpoint and bounded recovery}
\label{sec:criu}
Replay from process start costs time linear in the log length; a checkpoint bounds it to the
post-checkpoint tail. Two mechanisms provide checkpoints. An application-native
snapshot (a Redis RDB) plus tail-replay works for a single process; combined with a
virtual-clock tick checkpoint, the recovered instance resumes time exactly where the
snapshot was taken. The general, any-binary mechanism is CRIU~\cite{criu}: it checkpoints the
whole process---memory, file descriptors, threads, \emph{and} the in-memory virtual
clock---so a restore needs no replay of the pre-checkpoint history. We verify CRIU
checkpoint/restore of the hardest app class, a multi-process PostgreSQL (postmaster
plus background workers, SysV and POSIX shared memory, WAL): data is byte-identical
across the crash and the restored server is live (\S\ref{sec:eval-recovery}).
We are precise about what PostgreSQL demonstrates and what it does not: it exercises
the \emph{checkpoint/restore} path, not deterministic replay. A multi-process
shared-everything database has cross-process non-determinism (shared-buffer access
order across backends) that neither share-nothing sharding nor a per-process virtual
clock removes; PostgreSQL therefore lives in the \emph{checkpoint-only} regime---
recovered entirely from a CRIU image, with no order-log-free tail replay---which is
the regime Remus and HyCoR also occupy. Deterministic-replay recovery is demonstrated
on the four single-thread servers; shared-everything deterministic replay is out of
scope (\S\ref{sec:limits}). The checkpoint itself is a stop-the-world operation: the
quiesce-at-$T$ pause is bounded by the in-flight-handler drain plus the image write,
and for CRIU the image write is the dominant term ($37$--$121$\,ms for $30$--$300$\,MB
of RSS, \S\ref{sec:eval-compete}); the snapshot interval trades this steady pause
against recovery time (\S\ref{sec:eval-compete}), and an application-native
incremental snapshot (a Redis RDB) shortens it where available.

\subsection{Output suppression and exactly-once}
On replay the server re-emits outputs it already sent before the crash. The libOS
suppresses these via a per-client \texttt{(client, seq)} high-water-mark: an output
whose sequence is at or below the client's last acknowledged sequence is dropped, so
each acknowledged effect is externalized exactly once across recovery. The
high-water-mark map is itself durable: it is captured in every timestamp-$T$ snapshot
and reconstructed by the same dedup fold that replays the log, so suppression survives
a crash that loses all volatile state. The exactly-once and no-lost-acknowledged-write
invariants this yields are machine-checked in TLA+ (\S\ref{sec:impl},
Appendix~\ref{app:tla}), whose \textsc{AckedDurable} invariant encodes the
output-commit precondition: an output is released only after its producing input is in
the snapshot or the durable log.

\paragraph{Engineering realities.} Making ``unmodified'' credible required handling
the awkward edges of interposition: the \texttt{LD\_PRELOAD} early-init trap (the
dynamic loader calls \texttt{pthread\_mutex\_lock} before the shim's constructor
resolves it); glibc symbol versioning (\texttt{pthread\_cond\_wait} must be bound
to its \texttt{GLIBC\_2.3.2} version via \texttt{dlvsym}, or threaded servers hang);
and the seccomp filter that traps a syscall while the supervisor that services the
trap runs in the same process. These are not incidental---they are why prior
transparent systems that intercept at the library level miss the raw-syscall and
internal-call paths that real servers actually use.

\section{Fault Tolerance over the Fabric}
\label{sec:ft}

The engine that ties the libOS to the fabric is a deterministic-replay state
machine with a durable ordered log and timestamp-$T$ snapshots
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:ft}). On the live path it applies fabric-delivered inputs in
\texttt{msg\_ts} order, appends \texttt{(ts, op)} to the durable log, and---when the
commit barrier passes a snapshot timestamp $T$---takes an uncoordinated
empty-channel checkpoint (point (b) of \S\ref{sec:overview}). Each input is
scattered to the backup replicas in one RTT (1Pipe's replication), and an output is
released only once the reliable-delivery commit barrier has passed its producing
input's timestamp---i.e., once that input prefix is replicated (point (c) of
\S\ref{sec:overview}). On recovery, a replacement replica loads the latest snapshot,
replays the durable log suffix in timestamp order (no order-log), and state-transfers
any prefix it is still missing by importing a surviving replica's state up to the live
cut; output suppression via the per-client high-water-mark makes the replayed effects
exactly-once.

\paragraph{Membership, durability, and recovery dynamics.} The configuration is $k$
replicas; each input is scattered to all $k$ at its total-order position, so it is
durable once held by any survivor. The guarantee is in-memory $f$-of-$k$ fail-stop
tolerance: an acknowledged write survives up to $f$ correlated failures as long as one
of the $k-f$ survivors holds the replicated prefix. Membership rides on the fabric:
1Pipe's failure detector (tens of microseconds at the operating point) excises a dead
peer, after which the survivors' commit barrier resumes over the reduced membership;
we validate on the live fabric that with $k\!=\!3$ a mid-stream crash leaves the
survivors at the exact expected state (total order held across the crash) and the
victim recovers a consistent prefix and catches up. Recovery is not unconditional: a
replica that must replay while the live stream advances converges only while its replay
rate exceeds the live arrival rate; at sustained peak load the naive scheme livelocks,
and the fabric's barrier-hold backpressure (throttling senders toward the recovering
node) restores convergence (\S\ref{sec:eval-time}). What we do \emph{not} build is an
automated primary-promotion / view-change layer: we validate the recovery
\emph{mechanism}---restore, replay, and state-transfer to the live cut---and inherit
membership from the fabric, but a production leader-election protocol that selects
which survivor is promoted is engine-level future work (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\small
\caption{OneBarrier live and recovery paths (per replica).}
\label{alg:ft}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Procedure{OnDeliver}{$(\mathit{ts}, \mathit{op})$}\Comment{fabric delivers in \texttt{ts} order}
  \State $\mathit{log}.\textsc{append}(\mathit{ts}, \mathit{op})$;\quad $\mathit{out} \gets \textsc{Apply}(\mathit{op})$
  \If{$\mathit{out}.\mathit{seq} > \mathit{hwm}[\mathit{out}.\mathit{client}]$}\Comment{exactly-once suppression}
     \State scatter input to replicas (1 RTT); release $\mathit{out}$ at commit barrier
     \State $\mathit{hwm}[\mathit{out}.\mathit{client}] \gets \mathit{out}.\mathit{seq}$
  \EndIf
\EndProcedure
\Procedure{OnBarrier}{$T$}\Comment{commit barrier passes snapshot point $T$}
  \State quiesce inputs $> T$; drain handlers $\le T$; \textsc{Checkpoint}($T$)\Comment{empty-channel cut}
\EndProcedure
\Procedure{Recover}{}
  \State \textsc{Load}(latest snapshot); \textbf{replay} $\mathit{log}$ suffix in \texttt{ts} order
  \State import a survivor's state for any prefix still missing, up to the live cut
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

The OneBarrier engine is $\sim$4.8\,k lines of Rust over the \texttt{ReliableHost}
fabric of an open 1Pipe reproduction, implementing the key/value state machine, the
durable ordered log, timestamp-$T$ snapshots, exactly-once output suppression, and
crash recovery; the production servers (RESP/Redis, Memcached, HTTP, a transactional
store, a pub/sub log), the fault injectors, and the correctness checkers ship as
runnable binaries, and each evaluation result is guarded by a named unit test that
asserts the property under test (for instance, that the passive design uses less
execution CPU than active SMR, and that OneBarrier writes no order-log where HyCoR
does). The libOS is three
\texttt{LD\_PRELOAD} C libraries (\texttt{libobpreload} for sockets/time/capture,
\texttt{librngdet} for the seccomp randomness trap, \texttt{libdetsched} for
deterministic scheduling). We machine-check the core protocols in TLA+: 1Pipe total
order (TLC explored $3.5\times10^6$ states with no order violation) and OneBarrier's
exactly-once and no-lost-acknowledged-write properties. The TLA+ models verify the
\emph{protocol abstractions}---the ordering rule and the replay-with-dedup engine---not
the Rust and C implementations; the implementation is validated separately by the
crash-injection and linearizability checks of \S\ref{sec:eval-correct}. Every result in
\S\ref{sec:eval} is reproduced by a single command recorded in the artifact.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

We lead with the results measured on real, unmodified binaries, then quantify the
barrier coincidence \S\ref{sec:overview} establishes by construction. Do real unmodified
servers recover byte-identically (\S\ref{sec:eval-recovery})? Are they correct under
fault (\S\ref{sec:eval-correct})? How fast is recovery (\S\ref{sec:eval-time})? What
does the libOS cost (\S\ref{sec:eval-overhead})? What is the measured magnitude of the
output-commit/commit-barrier coincidence (\S\ref{sec:eval-overlap})? And how does the
resource cost compare to prior transparent systems (\S\ref{sec:eval-compete})?

\subsection{Byte-identical recovery of five unmodified servers}
\label{sec:eval-recovery}
Four of the five servers exercise the \emph{deterministic-replay} path; the fifth,
PostgreSQL, exercises the general \emph{checkpoint/restore} path (\S\ref{sec:criu}).
For the four, we record an unmodified server under the virtual clock and randomness
stack, crash it with \texttt{kill -9}, wait a multi-second \emph{real} wall-clock gap,
and replay on a fresh instance with the same persisted base; a control instance runs
without the libOS so its real-time output must differ. Table~\ref{tab:apps} summarizes:
every app's time-dependent output is byte-identical across the gap, and the control
differs. Beyond observable outputs, full application \emph{state} reconstructs
identically: a Redis store with TTL'd keys recovers byte-identical absolute
expiries; a Node.js session store keyed by \texttt{Math.random()} with
\texttt{Date.now()} timestamps recovers identical session IDs and timestamps; and
Memcached's LRU \emph{eviction} decisions under memory pressure recover bit-exactly.
PostgreSQL is the fifth and hardest target, but recovers by a different mechanism: a
multi-process shared-everything database is not a deterministic-replay candidate
(\S\ref{sec:criu}), so we recover it through a CRIU checkpoint/restore of its full
multi-process tree---byte-identical table contents, restored server accepting new
writes---validating the checkpoint path on the hardest app class rather than the
order-log-free replay claim.

\subsection{Correctness under fault}
\label{sec:eval-correct}
We apply two distinct adversarial checks to a recovered \emph{unmodified} Redis. Eight
concurrent clients drive unique-key writes and a shared register; mid-load we
\texttt{kill -9} the server and recover it by replaying the captured request stream.
The first check is durability survival: every one of $191{,}073$ acknowledged writes
survived recovery with its exact value ($0$ lost, $0$ torn); in-flight operations are
excluded as the honest output-commit gap. The second is a true consistency verdict on
the contended object: a from-scratch Wing--Gong
linearizability~\cite{herlihy1990linearizability} checker confirms that the concurrent
$33$-operation register history---including the post-recovery read---is linearizable.
The two are complementary and we do not conflate them: the $191$k figure is large-scale
survival of acknowledged effects, while the linearizability \emph{verdict} (a search
over real-time-overlapping operations, exponential in concurrency) is run on the small
shared-register history where it is decidable, and is a genuine verdict rather than an
acked-set heuristic.

\subsection{Recovery time and availability}
\label{sec:eval-time}
Recovery latency grows linearly with log length above a fixed restore floor, and
reconstruction is exact. Replaying an unmodified Redis's captured request stream on a
fresh instance takes $35$\,ms for $10$k requests, $87$\,ms for $100$k, $269$\,ms for
$500$k, and $536$\,ms for $1$M, with the recovered key count equal to the live count at
every size (Figure~\ref{fig:rectime}). The points fit an affine model---a $\sim$30\,ms
fixed cost (process spawn, connection setup, snapshot restore) plus a
$\sim$0.5\,ms-per-1000-request replay slope ($\sim$95\,MB/s, $\sim$1.9\,M req/s
asymptotically); at $10$k the fixed floor dominates, so small-log recovery should not be
read off the slope.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/fig_recovery_time.pdf}
\caption{Recovery time is affine in log length---a $\sim$30\,ms fixed restore floor
plus a $\sim$0.5\,ms-per-1000-request slope---and reconstruction is exact (recovered

A checkpoint bounds replay to the post-checkpoint tail: with a checkpoint

the seconds-to-minutes detection-and-restart of cluster failover.

\subsection{Steady-state overhead of the libOS}
\label{sec:eval-overhead}
On \texttt{redis-benchmark} and ApacheBench, time virtualization costs $\sim$2--4\%
and randomness virtualization is within noise; FT request capture costs $<\!5\%$ on
realistic Nginx load (and is, in the real design, the fabric's 1-RTT replica write
rather than a local log). The one expensive component is the deterministic
scheduler (\S\ref{sec:threads}), which is why the production path is share-nothing
single-thread shards: Memcached single-thread under the full libOS sustains
$302$\,k ops/s (a $\sim$10\% FT overhead over its $342$\,k baseline), and four such
shards aggregate to $1.0$\,M ops/s---exceeding one 4-worker process while remaining
deterministic (Figure~\ref{fig:overhead}). The aggregate ($1.0$\,M) is below four
times the single-shard figure ($4\!\times\!302$\,k) because all four shards and the
load generator share one host's cores and NIC, so per-shard throughput drops under
contention for shared resources; the comparison that matters is against the $821$\,k of
a 4-worker process on the \emph{same} host, which the shards beat without lock
contention and without losing determinism.

\subsection{Resource cost and competitors}
\label{sec:eval-compete}
We compare against in-harness implementations of the two mechanisms prior
transparent systems pay and OneBarrier avoids: HyCoR's per-event non-determinism
(order) log, and LLFT's host-software sequencer. With the same apply-and-append
work, OneBarrier outperforms HyCoR by $1.5$--$2.2\times$ (Figure~\ref{fig:competitors})
because it writes no order-log---the fabric supplies the order, and a deterministic
test confirms HyCoR persists strictly more durable bytes for the identical workload.
LLFT's host sequencer is \emph{masked} at the engine level by the dominant
apply-and-append work, so it tracks OneBarrier here; isolating ordering as the only
work exposes it---a central sequencer degrades under producer contention
($96\!\rightarrow\!12$\,M ops/s) where fabric/timestamp ordering scales, the
in-network advantage 1Pipe measures at $2$--$20\times$ on real hardware. Together
these bracket the contribution: both the order-log (HyCoR) and the sequencer (LLFT)
are costs the fabric removes.

OneBarrier's \emph{passive} design is also cheaper in execution CPU
(Figure~\ref{fig:resource}a): one live replica plus log-only backups uses
$\sim$1$\times$ execution CPU ($106$--$123$\,ms across $2$--$7$ replicas) where active
SMR grows linearly with the replica count ($206$--$730$\,ms), a $49$--$83\%$ saving,
because the backups never execute the state machine. Against a real CRIU checkpoint
competitor, log-based recovery avoids periodic full-RSS image writes (CRIU
checkpoint $37$--$121$\,ms for $30$--$300$\,MB; Figure~\ref{fig:recovery}b). The
snapshot interval trades the two off as the recovery model predicts
(Figure~\ref{fig:resource}b): a small interval triples steady apply cost but recovers
in $\sim$20\,\textmu s, a large one minimizes steady cost but recovers $283\times$
slower.

\section{Generality: Beyond the Five Servers}
\label{sec:ext}

The five servers of \S\ref{sec:eval-recovery} are all ``request/response store with
in-memory state.'' To test whether the mechanism is that narrow, we apply it to ten more
applications spanning five classes---message brokers, databases, a network function, a
microservice, and network-infrastructure servers---chosen by a fit-test that falls
directly out of the design: an application extends transparently if it is (i)
\emph{deterministic given input order}---the only residual non-determinism is the time,
randomness, and thread-scheduling the libOS virtualizes; (ii) \emph{share-nothing or
cleanly shardable}; (iii) \emph{socket-based}, so the libOS can route it through the
fabric; and (iv) bounded in \emph{output}, so output-commit can hold and dedup it.
Table~\ref{tab:ext} summarizes the result: every application that passes the test
recovers, and each is one command in the artifact.

\paragraph{Message brokers contest exactly-once streaming.} A broker's job---total order
plus durable delivery---is what the fabric already provides, so a broker is nearly free
over OneBarrier. A stock unmodified \texttt{redis-server} used as a stream broker
recovers its entry IDs and consumer offsets byte-identically across a crash, because a
Redis stream auto-ID is \texttt{<ms>-<seq>} derived from the server clock, which the
virtual clock determinizes (the streaming-log analog of the Nginx \texttt{Date} header).
The Kafka \emph{partition} model is exactly our share-nothing sharding: $N$ single-thread
partitions, each with its own durable log; a killed partition recovers byte-identically
with offsets preserved, and aggregate publish throughput scales near-linearly ($7.9\times$
at $8$ partitions). A third broker, stock Mosquitto, generalizes the result to the
MQTT/IoT protocol: its retained-message store recovers by replaying the publish stream,
and the broker uptime is deterministic under the virtual clock. This targets the
exactly-once stream processing (Flink) the rewrite path was built for.

\paragraph{A database on the replay path, not just the checkpoint path.} PostgreSQL
recovered only via CRIU (\S\ref{sec:criu}) because it is shared-everything. SQLite is
single-threaded and embeddable, so a stock \texttt{python3} process using the stdlib
\texttt{sqlite3} module recovers by order-log-free \emph{deterministic replay}: rows
whose values come from SQLite's own time (\texttt{strftime('now')}) and PRNG
(\texttt{random()}, seeded from \texttt{/dev/urandom}) are byte-identical across a crash
and a real-time gap, where a no-libOS control differs in both. This is the first SQL
database on the deterministic-replay path, and it shows the database boundary is
shared-everything concurrency, not ``databases.'' For the shared-everything DBs that
\emph{do} fall in the checkpoint-only regime, the general CRIU path covers both
architectures---PostgreSQL's multi-process tree (\S\ref{sec:criu}) and a single
multi-threaded MySQL/MariaDB, which CRIU checkpoints and restores byte-identically with
the server live.

\paragraph{Stateful network functions: FT as ordered replay.} An NF is a deterministic
packet-processing state machine, so its accumulated flow state is recoverable by
replaying the fabric-ordered packet log---the FTMB/Pico-Replication stateful-middlebox
 problem~\cite{sherry2015ftmb}. We run a software Click NF built from OpenClickNP
elements (a \texttt{FlowCache} learning table plus an L4 load-balancer) on the host CPU
under the libOS, fed packets over a socket so the virtual clock advances per packet. The
recovered flow table---each flow's backend affinity \emph{and} its conntrack last-seen
timestamp---is byte-identical to the pre-crash table; a control's timestamps differ,
isolating the libOS's role. A stateless NF restart cannot reconstruct this, which is the
whole problem stateful-NF-FT exists to solve.

\paragraph{Stateful microservices contest durable execution.} The durable-execution path
(Temporal, DBOS) rewrites a service to externalize its state and its non-deterministic
effects. OneBarrier gives an unmodified in-memory order/checkout microservice the same
guarantees transparently: across a crash, the order book recovers exactly-once (order
count and running total exact, no lost or duplicated orders) \emph{and} byte-identically,
including the random order ids and wall-clock timestamps a durable-execution rewrite
would have to externalize by hand; a control differs. The honest boundary is the one
\S\ref{sec:limits} already names: a service that calls \emph{other} non-cooperating
services cannot un-send those effects, so the clean wins are leaf and self-contained
services, not external-effect orchestrators.

\paragraph{Network-infrastructure servers, and a UDP generalization.} The ubiquitous
single-process infrastructure daemons fit directly. lighttpd and HAProxy recover their
time-derived HTTP \texttt{Date} header byte-identically across a crash, exactly as Nginx
does. dnsmasq, a caching DNS resolver, exposes its time-dependence through the cached
record TTL, which counts down as the clock advances; under the virtual clock the
remaining TTL is a deterministic function of the query sequence and recovers
byte-identically, where a control tracks real time. dnsmasq also exercised a boundary of
the original libOS: it serves DNS over \emph{UDP}, and the virtual clock advanced only on
TCP connection reads. We extended the libOS to tick on UDP datagram reception
(\texttt{recvfrom}/\texttt{recvmsg} on a bound socket), so single-process UDP servers are
deterministic on their native path; the change is six lines and left all prior results
unchanged. (dnsmasq's TCP path forks per query and resets the per-process tick counter,
so UDP is in fact the deterministic path---the opposite of the usual assumption.)

\paragraph{Cross-shard atomicity is the same primitive.} The obvious objection to
share-nothing sharding is multi-key atomicity. The fabric answers it for free: a 1Pipe
scattering delivers a group of messages to different destinations at one total-order
position, atomically (\S\ref{sec:why}), which is precisely a multi-shard transaction
without an application-level two-phase commit. The same primitive that makes a single
input durable across replicas makes a multi-shard write atomic across shards; we note
this as a design consequence the fabric enables rather than a measured result.

\section{What Is New: An Honest Reckoning}
\label{sec:novelty}

An adversarial prior-art review finds the conceptual thesis substantially
anticipated, and we state it plainly (Table~\ref{tab:prior}). LLFT~\cite{zhao2013llft}
already achieved transparent, order-log-free FT of unmodified socket applications
by tagging messages with totally-ordered virtual time; HyCoR~\cite{zhou2021hycor} already
did checkpoint-replay of unmodified containers; NOPaxos/Eris/Derecho
\cite{li2016nopaxos,li2017eris,jha2019derecho} already offloaded total order to the network for
\emph{active} SMR; and Remus/VMware-FT~\cite{cully2008remus,scales2010vmwareft} are the transparent
VM-FT systems whose output-hold latency we remove. The blunt objections are: ``it is
passive SMR and the vanishing order-log is Schneider's 36-year-old property''; ``LLFT
already did transparent no-order-log FT---you just substitute an in-network fabric
for the order source''; and ``the fabric is a network, not stable storage, so
output-commit needs a durability the fabric does not provide.''

What survives is not a new primitive but a realization-and-measurement: OneBarrier
is the first system to show that routing unmodified share-nothing servers through a
single in-network total-order reliable fabric makes transparent passive FT cost
$\approx$ the total-order baseline---by replicating the recovery log within the
fabric's reliable-delivery commit barrier---and to demonstrate the enabling
determinism libOS on five real unmodified servers including a multi-process database.
The defense against the durability objection is the in-fabric replica: each input is
scattered to it in one RTT (1Pipe's native replication) and is durable by the time
the very commit barrier that delivers the input reliably has passed, so the
coincidence is complete for the $f$-of-$k$ fail-stop guarantee we claim. The defense against the
``just LLFT'' objection is the operating-point argument and its measurement: LLFT
and the others lived at the millisecond scale where the technique's overhead was a
large fraction of latency; the contribution is showing that at the microsecond,
in-network-order operating point the cost \emph{structure} collapses and a
long-dismissed technique becomes practical.

\section{Limitations and Scope}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{No RDMA/P4 hardware.} The fabric's RDMA \emph{latency} is reproduced in a model
at the published operating point and corroborated by CPU-bound SoftRoCE verbs, so the
overlap is established by the model rather than measured on hardware; a hardware
RDMA/P4 testbed is the single result that would most strengthen the paper, and it is
the only major claim not measured on real artifacts.

\textbf{Durability model.} Durability is in-memory $f$-of-$k$ fail-stop tolerance via
in-fabric replication, not crash-consistent persistence across correlated power loss
(the FaRM/RAMCloud tradeoff~\cite{dragojevic2014farm}); the out-of-regime
fsync/cross-AZ design point stacks durability on the critical path and is not our
target.

\textbf{No automated failover layer.} We validate the recovery \emph{mechanism}
(restore, order-log-free replay, and state-transfer to the live cut) and inherit
membership from the fabric's failure detector, but we do not build the
primary-promotion / view-change protocol that a production deployment needs to select
and install a new live replica after a primary failure; that election layer is
engine-level future work (\S\ref{sec:ft}).

\textbf{Shared-everything determinism.} The order-log-free deterministic-replay
guarantee is for \emph{share-nothing} servers. Arbitrary shared-everything
multithreading---including a multi-process shared-memory database such as
PostgreSQL---is the deterministic-multithreading frontier; for those, our share-nothing
sharding does not apply and the deterministic scheduler's serialization cost is
prohibitive, so we fall back to the checkpoint-only path (CRIU, \S\ref{sec:criu}),
which provides FT without order-log-free replay.

\textbf{Residual non-determinism.} Time and randomness are virtualized through the
libc, syscall, and device-file surfaces, but two userspace instructions take no syscall
and export no symbol: \texttt{RDRAND} (closed for OpenSSL via \texttt{OPENSSL\_ia32cap})
and \texttt{RDTSC} (the timestamp counter, used by none of our five servers for
externally-visible time but not yet generally pinned). A fully general guarantee would
trap both---\texttt{RDTSC} via \texttt{prctl(PR\_SET\_TSC)}---and emulate them from the
virtual clock/seed; we leave this hardening as engineering, having shown it is
unnecessary for the five servers measured.

\textbf{Checkpoint pause and external effects.} The timestamp-$T$ snapshot is
stop-the-world: its pause is bounded by the in-flight drain plus the image write (CRIU
$37$--$121$\,ms for $30$--$300$\,MB, \S\ref{sec:eval-compete}), traded against recovery
time by the snapshot interval; we do not separately measure the intra-checkpoint tail
latency, and an incremental application-native snapshot reduces the pause where
available. Finally, transparent FT cannot recover external side effects already
delivered to non-cooperating peers; output commit bounds, but does not eliminate, the
externally-visible inconsistency window.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Transparent fault tolerance.} Whole-machine replication makes
unmodified workloads fault-tolerant, a line that begins with Bressoud and Schneider's
hypervisor-based fault tolerance~\cite{bressoud1995hypervisor}, which replays an
unmodified guest deterministically on a backup---the direct ancestor of the
output-hold and lock-step costs we attack. Remus~\cite{cully2008remus} checkpoints a VM
and buffers output; VMware FT~\cite{scales2010vmwareft} runs a lock-step backup;
COLO~\cite{dong2013colo} releases output while primary and backup agree; and Crane
\cite{cui2015crane} makes Paxos transparent by combining input determinism with
deterministic multithreading. Closest to us, LLFT~\cite{zhao2013llft} tags messages
with totally-ordered virtual time so an unmodified socket application replays with
no order-log, and HyCoR~\cite{zhou2021hycor} (with its peer-reviewed successor RRC
\cite{zhou2022rrc}) gives checkpoint-plus-replay FT for unmodified containers while
still logging non-determinism over a normal network. OneBarrier relocates both order
and durability into an in-network fabric, removing the order-log entirely.

\paragraph{Deterministic replay.} ReVirt~\cite{dunlap2002revirt}, rr
\cite{ocallahan2017rr}, and Castor~\cite{mashtizadeh2017castor} record
non-determinism for replay; the dominant cost is logging shared-memory access order.
OneBarrier sources order from the fabric and virtualizes only the residual
\emph{local} non-determinism---time, randomness, and single-thread
scheduling---of an unmodified server.

\paragraph{State-machine replication and in-network ordering.} SMR
\cite{schneider1990smr} feeds deterministic replicas a totally-ordered input
established by consensus (Paxos~\cite{lamport1998parttime}, Raft
\cite{ongaro2014raft}). A recent line offloads ordering to the network for
\emph{active} SMR---NOPaxos~\cite{li2016nopaxos}, Eris~\cite{li2017eris}, Derecho
\cite{jha2019derecho}---using programmable switches~\cite{bosshart2014p4} and logical
clocks~\cite{lamport1978time}. 1Pipe~\cite{li2021onepipe} instead provides scalable
total-order \emph{communication}, which we repurpose as a \emph{passive}-FT
substrate.

\paragraph{Durable execution and stream processing.} The industrial path rewrites
applications to externalize state: durable-execution engines (Temporal
\cite{temporal}, DBOS~\cite{skiadopoulos2021dbos}) and exactly-once stream
processors (Flink~\cite{carbone2015flink}). Deterministic databases take the same
order-establishes-determinism insight into the storage layer: Calvin~\cite{thomson2012calvin}
sequences transactions up front so replicas execute identically with no replica
coordination---the database-layer analogue of OneBarrier's fabric-supplied order, but
requiring an application rewrite. OneBarrier targets the unmodified fleet these paths
leave behind.

\paragraph{Checkpoint, deterministic multithreading, and RDMA.} CRIU~\cite{criu}
checkpoints unmodified processes---we use it for bounded recovery---as do fast
microVM snapshot systems~\cite{agache2020firecracker,ustiugov2021reap}. Kendo
\cite{olszewski2009kendo} and dThreads~\cite{liu2011dthreads} make multithreading
deterministic; their throughput cost on contended servers motivates our
share-nothing sharding, and execute-then-verify replication---Eve~\cite{kapritsos2012eve}
and Rex~\cite{guo2014rex}---reaches multi-core replication only by relaxing strict
determinism, the same wall we sidestep by sharding. The microsecond operating point is
set by RDMA
systems---FaRM~\cite{dragojevic2014farm}, whose in-memory-replication-vs-power-loss
durability tradeoff we adopt, and eRPC~\cite{kalia2019erpc}.

\paragraph{Library OS and kernel-bypass I/O.} SocksDirect~\cite{li2019socksdirect}
is the lineage of our user-space socket interception. A transparent libOS over an
RDMA fabric must wait on completions without busy-polling a core per connection;
FastWake~\cite{li2023fastwake} gives thread-per-connection RDMA servers
polling-class latency \emph{while sharing cores} via fast interrupt-mode wakeup---the
completion-wait mechanism that keeps our passive design's CPU cost and core-sharing
(\S\ref{sec:threads}) intact at the microsecond operating point. Arrakis
\cite{peter2014arrakis} and Demikernel~\cite{zhang2021demikernel} give applications
fast datapaths.

\paragraph{Output commit, clocks, and correctness.} Optimistic recovery and the
output-commit problem originate with Strom and Yemini~\cite{strom1985optimistic};
consistent distributed snapshots with Chandy and Lamport~\cite{chandy1985snapshots}.
Synchronized clocks enable consistent snapshots and external consistency: Spanner/
TrueTime~\cite{corbett2012spanner} and Huygens~\cite{geng2018huygens}. We verify
recovery against linearizability~\cite{herlihy1990linearizability}.

\section{Conclusion}

Transparent fault tolerance for unmodified servers failed to ship not because
snapshots are hard but because three coupled costs---order logging, the distributed
cut, and output commit---were large at the millisecond operating point. We showed
the verdict is operating-point-dependent: a user-space determinism libOS makes
unmodified Redis, Memcached, Nginx, Node.js, and PostgreSQL deterministically
recoverable at a few-percent overhead, and routing them through a microsecond
in-network total-order reliable fabric collapses all three costs---the fabric is the
order, a timestamp-$T$ snapshot replaces the distributed cut, and the output-commit
write folds into the fabric's commit barrier. At this operating point, transparent
fault tolerance becomes a byproduct of the communication fabric.

\section{Machine-Checked Specifications}
\label{app:tla}
We specify and model-check the two correctness-critical protocols in TLA+ with the
TLC model checker. \texttt{OnePipeTotalOrder} models the fabric's ordering and
checks \emph{total order}---every receiver delivers messages in one global order
consistent with the timestamps, with no observed inversion---over $3.5\times10^{6}$
reachable states with no violation. \texttt{OneBarrierEngine} models the replay
engine and checks two invariants under arbitrary crash/recover interleavings:
\textsc{ExactlyOnce} (each acknowledged client write is applied to the recovered

(every acknowledged write is present after recovery). The specifications are in the
artifact under \texttt{spec/}.
